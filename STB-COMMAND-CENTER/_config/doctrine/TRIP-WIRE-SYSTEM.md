# The Trip Wire System
**STB Canonical Doctrine · Layer 3 Reference · Stage 05 Input**

---

## What a Trip Wire Is

A trip wire is a moment when a practitioner's forward motion stops and the cause is not obvious.

It is not laziness. It is not failure. It is not a sign that the framework isn't working. It is a specific type of resistance that has a specific type, and the correct response to a trip wire depends entirely on correctly identifying what type it is.

The most dangerous operator error is prescribing the wrong response to the right signal. Pushing harder into a somatic trip wire produces shutdown. Offering emotional support into a logistical trip wire wastes time and trust. Correct identification comes first. Always.

---

## The Six Trip Wire Types

### Type 1 — Somatic

**What it looks like:**
The practitioner goes quiet. Stops responding to check-ins. Says "I've been meaning to" without doing the thing. Feels physically tired or heavy when thinking about the task. Reports feeling "frozen" or "unable to start" without being able to explain why.

**What it is:**
The nervous system has registered the task as unsafe — not dangerous, but outside the window of tolerance. This is a physiological response, not a psychological one. The body has vetoed the action before the mind has processed it.

**The correct immediate response:**
Do not address the task. Address the state.
- "Something seems to have shifted — how are you doing, not with the task, but in general?"
- Offer a smaller version of the task, not a pep talk about the original one
- The smallest possible action that is still in the direction of the task
- Rest is sometimes the correct prescription. Build in recovery time before the next ask.

**What makes it worse:**
Urgency. Accountability pressure. "You said you were going to do this." Any language that adds weight to an already overloaded nervous system will produce deeper shutdown.

---

### Type 2 — Identity

**What it looks like:**
The practitioner starts questioning whether they're the right person for this. "Is this really what I'm supposed to be doing?" "Maybe I should pivot." "I don't know if this is actually me." Revisits decisions that were already made. Compares themselves to others doing different things.

**What it is:**
The task has bumped against a belief about who they are or who they're allowed to be. Often triggered by: naming the offer publicly for the first time, raising prices, claiming an expertise, or taking an action that makes the business feel "real" in a way that raises the stakes of potential failure.

**The correct immediate response:**
Do not try to resolve the identity question. Anchor to evidence.
- "What have you actually done in the last 14 days?" (Evidence log)
- "What did your last client say when they finished working with you?"
- Return to the smallest confirmed truth: "You have helped [X] people do [Y]. That part is not in question."
- One small external confirmation (a testimonial, a message from a past client) is worth more than any amount of internal pep talk.

**What makes it worse:**
Big-picture visioning exercises. "Where do you want to be in five years?" when someone is questioning whether they belong here at all pushes them further into the spiral. Stay small and concrete.

---

### Type 3 — Relational

**What it looks like:**
Progress stalls around an action that involves another person — sending the email, making the ask, posting publicly, reaching out to a lead. The task gets done internally (the offer is written, the email is drafted) but never sent.

**What it is:**
Fear of how a specific person or group will respond. Often: fear of disappointing someone they respect, fear of being perceived as too commercial by their wellness community, fear of a specific person seeing the work and judging it. Sometimes: a real relationship dynamic (a partner who is sceptical, a family member who has expressed doubt) is operating in the background.

**The correct immediate response:**
Name the relationship, not the task.
- "Is there a specific person whose reaction you're anticipating when you think about doing this?"
- "What's the worst thing someone you care about could say about this, and how likely is that actually?"
- Sometimes: offer to review the thing-they-can't-send together before they send it. The barrier is often the aloneness of the action, not the action itself.

**What makes it worse:**
Treating it as a logistical problem. "Just send it" misses the relational dynamic entirely and erodes trust. They already know they could just send it.

---

### Type 4 — Logistical

**What it looks like:**
The practitioner wants to move forward but something concrete is in the way — they don't have the tool, they don't know how to use the tool, they're waiting on someone else, the tech isn't working, they don't know which option to choose.

**What it is:**
A real, solvable practical obstacle. This is the simplest trip wire type and the most frequently misdiagnosed as something deeper.

**The correct immediate response:**
Solve the logistical problem. Directly.
- "What specifically are you waiting on / don't know how to do?"
- Provide the answer, the workaround, or a different path that doesn't require the obstacle
- Do not turn this into an emotional conversation — it doesn't need to be one

**What makes it worse:**
Treating it as a psychological block. If someone is stuck because they don't know how to set up a payment link, they don't need a mindset conversation — they need to know how to set up a payment link. Over-psychologising a logistical problem wastes time and can make the practitioner feel more fragile than they are.

---

### Type 5 — Clarity

**What it looks like:**
"I'm not sure which one to do." "I have a few different ideas." "I keep going back and forth between X and Y." Multiple options are in play and no decision is being made. Action has stalled at the decision point.

**What it is:**
Insufficient clarity about the next step — either because the options are genuinely equivalent and a choice needs to be made, or because the practitioner is using optionality as a way to defer commitment.

**The correct immediate response:**
Narrow. Actively.
- "Of those three options, which one would you start if the others didn't exist?"
- "Which of these has the most evidence of working?" (Return to the evidence log)
- Make the call with them if they're genuinely stuck: "Based on what I know, I'd start with X. Here's why. Do you want to try that for the next 7 days?"
- Give them one thing to do, not a decision framework

**What makes it worse:**
More options. More research. "Let's explore all the possibilities." Clarity trips wires resolve with narrowing, not expanding. Adding information to a clarity block produces more paralysis, not less.

---

### Type 6 — Consent

**What it looks like:**
The practitioner feels like the system is doing something to them rather than with them. Expresses discomfort with the pace, the direction, or a specific task without being able to fully articulate why. May say "I don't know, something just doesn't feel right." May comply but with noticeably less energy. May start doing the tasks slightly differently than specified.

**What it is:**
The practitioner's agreement to the direction is incomplete. Something was agreed to that they didn't fully say yes to — either because they felt pressure to proceed, because they didn't feel they had the option to say no, or because the framework moved faster than their internal process could follow.

**The correct immediate response:**
Stop. Check consent explicitly.
- "I want to check — are you actually on board with this direction, or did you agree to it because you felt like you should?"
- "Is there something about this that doesn't fit for you that you haven't said yet?"
- Consent before extraction. If the practitioner hasn't fully said yes to the current stage, the next stage will produce work they don't stand behind.

**What makes it worse:**
Continuing to build on incomplete consent. Everything built on a consent trip wire will eventually have to be rebuilt. Slowing down here saves time later.

## The Correct Trip Wire Protocol

**Step 1 — Detect**
The practitioner has stopped moving. Something has changed. Don't assume which type it is.

**Step 2 — Name it without labelling it**
"I notice you haven't moved on [task] since [date]. That's useful information. Not a problem — just data. Can you tell me more about where things are?"

**Step 3 — Listen for the type**
From what they say, identify the most likely trip wire type. If it's unclear, ask one clarifying question before proceeding.

**Step 4 — Apply the type-specific response**
Match the response to the type. Not the response you'd want to give, or the response that feels most helpful in the abstract.

**Step 5 — Smallest possible action**
Whatever the next step is, make it smaller than you think it needs to be. The goal of the trip wire response is to restore motion, not to make up for lost time.

**Step 6 — Document in the evidence log**
A trip wire detected and resolved is evidence of the system working, not evidence of failure. Log it. It may be part of a pattern worth surfacing to the practitioner later.

## Application to the STB Command Center

**Practitioner-facing trip wire button (Sprint Board):**
"Something hit a wall" — teal ember button at the bottom of the Sprint board.

When tapped, the practitioner is NOT asked to diagnose their own trip wire type. They are asked one question:
- "What's the thing that stopped? Describe it in one sentence."

That response is passed to the AI layer (Stage 05) with the task context (`task_name`, `leak_zone`, `practitioner_id`, `last_3_evidence_logs`).

**Claude's trip wire response (Stage 05 — AI layer):**
Claude reads the description and the context, loads this doctrine file in full, identifies the most likely trip wire type, and returns:
1. The type (internal only — never shown to practitioner as a clinical label)
2. The correct immediate response (a single question or action for the operator to use)
3. The smallest possible next action in the direction of the original task
4. A flag for the operator view if the trip wire suggests a pattern (e.g., second consent trip wire in one sprint)

**Operator view (Stage 06):**
Trip wire flags appear in the operator's trip wire inbox. Operator sees: practitioner name, task that stalled, identified type, suggested response. Operator makes the final call on how to respond.

**The rule:**
Claude identifies the type. The operator confirms. The practitioner receives only the response — never the diagnosis. A practitioner should never feel like they are being clinically assessed. They should feel like someone who understands them is checking in.

## The Language of the Trip Wire Response

**Never say:**
- "You're experiencing resistance."
- "This is a somatic response."
- "You're stuck in an identity trip wire."
- "You need to work through this block."

**Always say:**
- "Something shifted — can you tell me more?"
- "That makes sense. Here's what I'd suggest."
- "Let's make this smaller. What's one thing you could do today?"
- "You don't need to solve the whole thing. Just the next stone."
